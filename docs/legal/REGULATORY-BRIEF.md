# Regulatory brief: can the benefit be in kind, and what authorisation do we need?

*Research, not legal advice. Prepared from the regulators' own published text where I could read it, and from law-firm commentary where I could not. Anything marked **Not confirmed** needs the lawyer.*

## What the sources say

**1. The funeral class is defined in money terms.**
Joint Communication 7 of 2024 (Prudential Authority and FSCA), Annexure, para 1.3, sets out the Insurance Act classes. Funeral Class (Class 4) is: "Lump sum or, specified or determinable equal or unequal sums of money payable at specified intervals not exceeding an amount prescribed by the Prudential Authority to cover the cost associated with a funeral **or the rendering of a service** on the happening of a death event."
So the benefit is **money** (to cover a funeral, or a service). Groceries and livestock are goods, not money and not obviously a "service". Whether our bundle fits is the central legal question. *Not confirmed.*

**2. In-kind benefits are specifically an issue in the Risk class.**
Commentary on Joint Communication 4 of 2020 says a policy in the Risk class "would not be able to provide benefits in the form of a funeral service (in-kind benefit)", because the Risk class description provides only for lump-sum or monetary benefits. (Secondary source: Moonstone and 2026 commentary. I could not retrieve the Joint Communication itself, so the lawyer should read it.)

**3. Running a funeral policy requires an insurance licence, or an insurer behind you.**
JC7 of 2024, para 1.2: anyone conducting life insurance business, including funeral policies, "must be licensed as an insurer". Para 1.5: parlours underwritten by a licensed insurer may intermediate, and must be authorised as FSPs under FAIS.

**4. The routes for a business that isn't an insurer (JC7 of 2024, para 1.6).**
- (a) A cell captive, joint venture or partnering arrangement with an insurer.
- (b) A microinsurance licence, with the insurance business in a **separate legal entity** from the service business.
- (c) Consolidating with others to form a microinsurer.
Para 1.7: a business that provides **only funeral services** and no insurance-related or other financial service needs no financial-sector authorisation.

**5. The cap.** Prudential Standard GOI 7: a maximum of **R100,000 per life insured**, escalating annually with CPI (footnote 3 of the same annexure).

**6. Regulators are watching this.** JC7 of 2024, para 3.9: the Authorities are "aware of a high number of unlicensed entities, including funeral parlours, that are engaged in the self-underwriting of insurance policies and unauthorised collection of 'premiums'". The inter-regulatory review (stakeholder workshops planned for the first half of 2025) was meant to produce a lower-barrier licensing framework. I did not find its outcome. *Check whether the framework has changed since.*

**7. Policyholder Protection Rules, Rule 2A.10 (microinsurance and funeral).** A funeral policy may not require a cash benefit to be paid directly to a service provider (2A.10.1), and no administration or similar fee may be charged for a service or other non-monetary benefit (2A.10.3). (From the published rules; the lawyer should confirm how these apply to us.)

## What this means for us, in plain terms
- Collecting a monthly amount in exchange for a benefit on someone's death is very likely "insurance business" **whether the benefit is money or goods**. The pure "services only" exemption (para 1.7) does not look like it covers a recurring contribution for a future death benefit. *Not confirmed.*
- So we most likely need an **insurer behind the plan**, with TGN as an authorised intermediary (FAIS), **or** a microinsurance licence in a separate company. A licence takes capital, time and a compliance function we do not have.
- Because the funeral class describes money, the **safest design is that the insured benefit is cash**, with the groceries, livestock and funeral partners offered as how the family chooses to use it. Whether the bundle can be the benefit itself needs the insurer's and the lawyer's view.

## Options to put to the lawyer
1. **Underwritten plan.** A licensed insurer underwrites a funeral policy; TGN is an authorised intermediary and fulfils the bundle. Fastest and cheapest to start. Needs an insurer who agrees to the in-kind or cash-with-bundle design.
2. **Cell captive / joint venture** with an insurer. More control and a share of the profit and loss; needs more capital and a longer lead time.
3. **Microinsurance licence** in a separate company. The most control; the slowest and most expensive.
4. **Not insurance at all.** For example, a pre-paid goods or service product with no insurance risk. This probably doesn't fit a recurring plan paid before a death, but the lawyer should say so definitively.

## Questions to ask, in order
1. On these facts, is TGN conducting insurance business? Why or why not?
2. If yes: of options 1 to 3, which is realistic for a small start-up, and what would each cost and take?
3. Can the benefit be goods and livestock rather than money, in the funeral class? If not, can it be cash with a bundle option?
4. Who may collect the premium, and can the person paying differ from the policyholder and the covered people?
5. What can we say publicly before we are authorised? Can we run a waiting list?
